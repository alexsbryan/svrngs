svrngs — keep what your group shares on your own computers.

To build an app for your group, make a folder with an index.html in it:
~/svrngs/<group>/apps/<name>/index.html. Within seconds it is on everyone's
home page. Plain HTML and JavaScript, no build step: the page reads and
writes its entries with fetch('svrngs/acts'). Every file in the group's
folder goes to every member and stays in the group's record for good, as
every entry does, so try things in a group of your own first (`svrngs new`).
`svrngs guide` prints the whole guide; `svrngs check <folder>` says what in
an app's page svrngs won't load, and what in its schema.sql makes no
database, and what to change. Name the folder of the version it follows
after it, and it checks that the new schema.sql only adds to that one's.

  svrngs
      Open your home page, starting svrngs first if it isn't running here,
      or restarting it if an older svrngs is. svrngs then runs for every
      group you are in, apart from this terminal. With no terminal, as
      when your login runs it, svrngs runs right there instead.

  svrngs new <name> [--as <your name>] [--origin <url>] [--relay <name>]...
      Make a group called <name> and print its link: send it to the people
      you want in it. It works for a day, while this computer is on. The
      group's folder is put at ~/svrngs/<name>. Where svrngs runs here
      already, as on a computer it runs at login, that svrngs makes the
      group and this command leaves, running nothing.
      --as is the name the group knows you by; else your full name as this
      computer shows it, or its user name.
      --origin also prints its links on a page address, from now on, for
      people who open them in a browser. On a phone each app then opens at
      an address of its own under that page's host, which must serve the
      page at every name under it, as group.svrnme.sh does.
      --relay meets the group at your own relay, `svrngs relay <name>`, and
      at the one svrngs uses whenever yours does not answer: every computer
      its links let in meets it there too, a newcomer through the fallback
      the link also carries. Give it again for each relay the group may
      meet at.

  svrngs join <link> [--as <your name>] [--no-run]
      Join the group a link you were sent is for. The computer that sent it
      must be on. --as is as for `new`. The group's folder is put at ~/svrngs/<group>.
      Where svrngs runs here already, as on a computer it runs at login,
      that svrngs joins for you and this command leaves, running nothing.
      --no-run stops once you are in, rather than run.

  svrngs link <group> [--device] [--origin <url>] [--relay <url>]...
      Print a new link to <group>. Any link you sent before stops letting
      anyone new in; everyone it let in stays. <group> is its name, or the
      first 8 letters of its id when two groups share a name. --origin
      also prints it on a page address, from now on, as `new` does.
      --device makes it a link for another computer or phone of yours: it
      joins as you, asked no name.
      --relay changes where <group> meets (keeper only). Where svrngs runs
      here already, that svrngs makes the link, unless --origin is given.

  svrngs keep <port> -- <command>
      Keep what you lend on <port> running, even after a restart, from
      the command you started it with, in this folder: stop your copy,
      then put `svrngs keep <port> --` in front of that command. svrngs
      shows what it will run and where, and keeps it once you press
      Enter, and only if it then answers on <port>. On Linux it asks
      whether to start it before anyone logs in, which lets your account
      run programs while nobody is logged in; else it comes back when you
      log in. Nothing kept may be in a folder your groups sync, or be a
      file another account could change.

  svrngs run <name> --port <port> [--folder <folder>] -- <command>
      Run a command here (Linux or macOS) under <name>, as keep does: it
      shows what it will run and where, and keeps it once you press Enter,
      and only if it then answers on <port>. Share it separately with
      svrngs share.
      To stop the app, choose Stop under Already running here on your home
      page (`svrngs open`). Stop leaves its setup and files in place.

  svrngs share <port> --group <group>... [--plan] [--name <name>] [--program <name>] [--pid <id>@<start>] [--replace] [--limit <n>] [--refuse <path>]... [--upload <Mbit/s>] [--reads <program>]... [--ask <words>] [--sign-in-with-svrngs] [--readers] [--udp | --tcp] [--ask-after-restart]
      Lend what runs on <port> on this computer to everyone in each
      --group, by its name or the start of its id; say it again for more.
      Each reaches it through this computer, as themselves. It asks for the
      password the service takes on each request, if any; that stays on
      this computer, and nobody you lend it to receives it. For a service
      it knows keeps its own accounts it asks none, and says how to make
      each person an account there: each signs in once. For one it knows
      takes each person from svrngs (Navidrome on 4533, Calibre-Web on
      8083) it asks none, and says what to set in it. --name is what
      they see it as; else its program's name. --program says which
      program it is, when svrngs does not know the port; without it, svrngs
      says the programs it knows. Of a program it knows, it keeps back, and
      says, what shows one person's use of it to the others or changes it
      for everyone. --limit is how
      many people may use it at once; else everyone in those groups. --refuse keeps a path of it from them; say it again
      for more. --upload is this computer's upload, in Mbit/s, as a speed
      test states it: each person using it is told their share of it.
      --reads lets it open, as whoever opens it, what your groups lend of
      <program>; say it again for more. --ask says, in your words,
      what an app receives from it: then an app uses it only once the
      person it acts for says yes, on their own home page, and stops when
      they say stop. --sign-in-with-svrngs lets an app that speaks OpenID
      Connect sign each person in as themselves, with nothing typed: it
      creates credentials, not the app's configuration. Follow the app's
      OpenID Connect setup guide; the secret stays on this computer.
      --readers asks for an account you made in it for your groups, or a
      key you made in it: apps that read it see what that account or key
      sees, and only look.
      --udp or --tcp lends a port that speaks UDP or TCP as it is, not
      as a page: it takes a --program, and --name and --limit as any lend
      does.
      A lend is kept to what answers on its port when you share it, and
      says what that is. svrngs checks the port before each connection it
      carries there, and before and after each batch of datagrams it sends
      to a UDP port. If the program starts again, the lend closes until you approve
      what runs there: `svrngs approve <name>`, or Keep sharing it on your
      home page. While another program is on the port, the lend is closed;
      a UDP lend stays closed after it goes, and when svrngs starts again,
      until you approve. --pid lends it only if that process, or a child of
      it, holds every socket that takes what svrngs sends to the port;
      <start> is its start, as /proc/<id>/stat's 22nd field says on Linux.
      A name you share from another port, or as another protocol, is
      refused unless you add --replace.
      At a terminal, svrngs shows who can use it, when it can come back,
      and whether it needs a restart before asking you to share. --plan
      shows the review alone. --ask-after-restart shares this run only;
      with no terminal, `share` shares this run only too.

  svrngs approve <name> [--pid <id>@<start>] [--token <token>] [--ask-after-restart]
      Say what answers on <name>'s port now, and keep sharing <name> with
      it once you type y. If something else took the port while you read,
      nothing is approved. Nothing else about it changes. --pid and
      --ask-after-restart are as for `share`. Without a terminal, it approves only with --token, the
      token a program read from the lend's row in svrngs's API, for what
      that row showed.

  svrngs unshare <name> [--group <group>]...
      Stop lending <name>; with --group, stop lending it to that group
      only, keeping the rest.

  svrngs open [/<local destination>] [--print]
      Open your home page in your browser, as svrngs in your Applications
      (on Linux, your applications menu) does, starting svrngs first if it
      isn't running. An optional local destination
      focuses that part of the home page. --print shows an address to open
      in another browser within a minute, once, and opens nothing.

  svrngs ask <route> [<json>]
      Print svrngs's answer to <route> as a program of yours gets it, as
      `svrngs ask /v1/groups` does; with <json>, post that there. It never
      sends svrngs's token: it checks first that what answers is your
      svrngs, then asks with a code good for that one connection. A script
      in any language can run it instead of reading the token. It exits 1,
      saying why, when svrngs refuses, and 3 when svrngs isn't running.

  svrngs update
      Get the newest svrngs from the page this computer was installed
      from, if this computer's is not it, and restart svrngs onto it.
      Where a package installed svrngs, the package updates it, and this
      says how.

  svrngs version
      Which svrngs this is: its release, and the commit it was built from.

  svrngs restart
      Stop svrngs and start it again, or start it if it isn't running.
      Any command that finds an older svrngs running here restarts it
      onto itself first.

  svrngs share --folder <dir> --group <group>
      Keep <dir> the same on every computer in <group> that shares a folder
      of its name, whenever svrngs runs here, from now on: anyone in it may
      add, change and delete in it, and nobody outside it receives a byte.

  svrngs room <name>...
      Open the lends named <name> to this computer's WiFi, so a TV, a phone
      or another computer here can open them as you. They stay open on that
      WiFi until you close them on your home page. Works whether or not
      svrngs is already running.

  svrngs remove <name> --group <group> [--joined <when>]
      Remove <name> from <group>: every computer and phone of theirs. One
      person decides who is removed — whoever made the group, until they
      hand that on — so from anyone else this asks them, and says who.
      What <name> already has stays with them; nothing new reaches them.
      Asked of svrngs running here. --joined says which of two people
      called <name> you mean: when they joined, as svrngs says it.

  svrngs handover <name> --group <group> [--joined <when>]
      Let <name> decide who is removed from <group> from now on, instead
      of you, once they accept it. Asked of svrngs running here. --joined
      as for remove.

  svrngs accept <group>
      Decide who is removed from <group>, when someone handed that to
      you; or take back your own handover before it is accepted. Asked
      of svrngs running here.

  svrngs leave <group>
      Leave <group> from every computer and phone of yours. What you have
      of it stays here; nothing new reaches you, nor what you lend them.
      Rejoining needs a new invitation, and not from this computer yet.
      If you decide who is removed, hand that on first.

  svrngs relay <name> [--open] [--staging]
      Relay at https://<name>/ for the computers and phones of the groups
      this computer is in, and for a phone opening a lend of theirs, beside
      svrngs: a phone reaches a computer only through a relay. <name> must
      lead to this computer, which must take
      connections on TCP ports 80 and 443 and UDP port 7842. Its
      certificate is asked of Let's Encrypt, and kept in the data below.
      The group's page's install line, with --relay <name> after the
      page's address, keeps it running.
      --open relays for every computer and phone that reaches it, as
      svrngs's own relay does, each held to the most a phone was measured
      drawing through a relay in a second; it says how much as it starts.
      --staging asks Let's Encrypt's trial service, whose certificates no
      browser trusts: to try <name> first.

Data:    $SVRNGS_DIR, else ~/.svrngs.
Logging: RUST_LOG, default `error`.
